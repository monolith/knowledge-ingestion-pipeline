## 1. Calling a market reaction an overreaction presupposes a norm for the correct reaction, and the norm De Bondt and Thaler adopt is Bayes' rule, which prescribes how beliefs should be revised in light of new information. This is what lets a psychological finding be turned into a testable market claim: without a normative benchmark, 'too much' reaction has no meaning.

**Role in the source.** Opens the paper's conceptual move: it fixes the benchmark before any empirical claim, which is what makes the rest testable.

## 2. People systematically violate Bayes' rule when revising beliefs: they overweight recent information and underweight prior or base-rate data. Kahneman and Tversky attribute this to a matching rule, in which a case's predicted value is chosen so that its standing among outcomes matches its standing among impressions -- an instance of the representativeness heuristic, which violates the principle that extreme predictions must be moderated by how predictable the outcome actually is.

**Role in the source.** The psychological premise the whole study imports; the market hypothesis is this finding transposed to prices.

- *primary* ✓ (lines 49–51): “In  revising their beliefs, individuals tend to overweight recent information and  underweight prior (or base rate) data.”

- *supporting* ✓ (lines 56–57): “violates the basic statistical principal that the extremeness of predictions must  be moderated by considerations of predictability”

grounding: `attributable` · decision: `keep` · quantitative: `False` · topics: `representativeness heuristic`, `Bayes' rule`, `base rate neglect`

## What the omission check said was missing

- **missing** — The five numbered estimation steps in Section I.A define the statistical machinery -- the pooled population-variance estimate across the 16 paired portfolios, the t-statistic on the difference of sample means, and the separate per-month test of whether a single average residual differs from zero. Every t-statistic quoted in the results depends on these definitions, and none of them is represented in a unit.
- **missing** — The paper states that the t-statistics for the 36 postformation months do not constitute independent evidence. This is a direct limitation on how the headline significance claims may be read and is absent from the extraction.
- **missing** — Footnote 1 records two live alternatives to the overreaction reading of Shiller's excess volatility: that price variability reflects changes in real interest rates (rejected because land and housing do not move with stocks), and Marsh and Merton's argument that Shiller misspecified the dividend process. The unit on excess volatility presents the overreaction reading without these competing explanations.
- **missing** — Footnote 6 reports that Reinganum, after purging the data of tax-loss selling effects, still finds a smaller January seasonal related to company size, and that this may be an artifact of his six-month definition of the tax-loss measure. This qualifies the unit claiming the small firm effect is redefined as a losing-firm effect.
- **missing** — The design is a variant on a procedure originally proposed by Beaver and Landsman in a different context, and the paper contrasts it explicitly with the standard semistrong-form test that forms portfolios on an event such as an earnings announcement. The provenance and the contrast are both absent.
- **missing** — Table I carries per-row results that no unit represents, including the five-year formation experiment (10 replications, 50 stocks) showing a CAR difference of 0.319 at 60 months with a t-statistic of 3.28 -- the largest and most significant reversal in the paper, and stronger than the three-year result the extraction treats as headline.
- **missing** — The five-year experiment is explicitly framed as having the properties of a trading rule -- the average cumulative excess return before transaction costs an investor aware of the phenomenon could expect. The extraction records the persistence of the January effect from this experiment but not its economic-significance framing or the before-transaction-costs caveat.
- **missing** — Because the same constant market return is subtracted from every stock's return, the results are interpretable in terms of raw dollar returns. This is what licenses reading the 19.6% and 24.6% figures as ordinary returns rather than model-relative quantities.
