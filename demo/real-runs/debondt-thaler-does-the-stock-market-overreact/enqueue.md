## 1. The winner-loser reversal (De Bondt and Thaler, 1985)

`create` · knowledge state **preliminary** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-001-r1` v2

**Slug** `the-winner-loser-reversal-de-bondt-and-thaler-1985`

### Summary

The paper's central finding: portfolios of stocks with the most extreme prior losses outperform portfolios of prior winners over the following three years, by an amount that grows with how extreme the prior move was. One study, one dataset, one independence group -- the figures below are a single test, not a replicated result, and the paper predates the replications and challenges that followed.

### Assertions (6)

1. Over the half-century to 1982, loser portfolios of 35 NYSE stocks outperformed the market by an average 19.6 per cent in the thirty-six months after portfolio formation, while winner portfolios earned about 5.0 per cent less than the market. The difference in cumulative average residual return between the extreme portfolios was 24.6 per cent, with a t-statistic of 2.20.

   *backed by* `asmt-0012`

2. The effect is asymmetric: it is much larger for losers than for winners.

   *backed by* `asmt-0012`

3. The more extreme the formation-period returns, the larger the subsequent reversal. Across seven specifications in Table I the difference in cumulative average residual grows with formation-period extremity; with a five-year formation period and 50-stock portfolios over 10 independent replications it reaches 0.319 at 60 months (t = 3.28), on a longer formation and test window than the headline experiment and so not directly comparable to the 24.6 per cent.

   *backed by* `asmt-0013`

4. Boundary condition, stated carefully because the source and its own table differ in emphasis: the paper says that for a formation period as short as one year 'no reversal is observed at all', while Table I reports the 49 one-year replications at -0.076 twelve months into the test period with a t-statistic of -2.32 -- a significant difference in the opposite direction, winners over losers. What is established is that the overreaction reversal does not appear at a one-year formation horizon; the negative sign is not explained by the paper.

   *backed by* `asmt-0013`

5. The effect occurs mostly in the second and third year of the test period. Twelve months into the three-year test the difference between the extreme portfolios is 5.4 per cent with a t-statistic of 0.77 -- not significant. A test run over one year would have found nothing.

   *backed by* `asmt-0013`

6. The conclusion states the result as: prior losers outperform prior winners, with the losing stocks earning about 25 per cent more thirty-six months after formation, even though the winners are significantly more risky.

   *backed by* `asmt-0015`

### Related topics

`weak-form market efficiency`, `contrarian investing`, `cumulative abnormal returns`

## 2. Overreaction: the behavioral premise behind the winner-loser reversal

`create` · knowledge state **supported** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-002-r1` v2

**Slug** `overreaction-the-behavioral-premise-behind-the-winner-loser-reversal`

### Summary

The psychological claim the market test was built to check, and the older observations of the same pattern in markets. Unlike the rest of this corpus the premise rests on several independent sources -- but they are reported here as De Bondt and Thaler characterize them; the primary studies are not in this corpus.

### Assertions (5)

1. Overreaction is defined against a norm rather than by intuition. Probability revision problems have a well-established norm because Bayes' rule prescribes the correct reaction to new information, and the term overreaction carries an implicit comparison to a degree of reaction considered appropriate.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. In revising their beliefs, individuals tend to overweight recent information and underweight prior or base-rate data. Kahneman and Tversky describe the operative rule of thumb as one where the predicted value is selected so that the standing of the case in the distribution of outcomes matches its standing in the distribution of impressions -- an instance of the representativeness heuristic, which violates the principle that the extremeness of predictions must be moderated by considerations of predictability.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The finding survives the two obvious objections: Grether replicated it under incentive-compatible conditions, and there is considerable evidence that the actual expectations of professional security analysts and economic forecasters display the same overreaction bias.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Excessive market reaction to short-run news was described decades before it was measured. Keynes held that day-to-day fluctuations in the profits of existing investments, obviously ephemeral and nonsignificant, tend to have an altogether excessive and even absurd influence on the market; Williams held that prices have been based too much on current earning power and too little on long-term dividend paying power; Arrow concluded that Kahneman and Tversky's work typifies very precisely the excessive reaction to current information that seems to characterize all securities and futures markets.

   *backed by* `asmt-0002`

5. These older statements are testimony, not measurement. Their value is showing the hypothesis predates the test, and they should not be counted as evidence for it.

   *backed by* `asmt-0002`

### Related topics

`representativeness heuristic`, `base-rate neglect`, `behavioral finance`

## 3. What the overreaction hypothesis predicts, and what it does not settle

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-003-r1` v2

**Slug** `what-the-overreaction-hypothesis-predicts-and-what-it-does-not-settle`

### Summary

The two testable predictions, the standard the authors set for themselves, and the theoretical question they explicitly leave open.

### Assertions (4)

1. Prediction 1: extreme movements in stock prices will be followed by subsequent price movements in the opposite direction. Prediction 2: the more extreme the initial price movement, the greater will be the subsequent adjustment. Both imply a violation of weak-form market efficiency, because if prices systematically overshoot the reversal is predictable from past return data alone, with no accounting data such as earnings.

   *backed by* `asmt-0006`

2. The authors set their own standard: to test whether the overreaction hypothesis is predictive -- whether it does more than explain, ex post, the P/E effect or Shiller's results -- and claim these are the first tests to use a behavioral principle to predict a new market anomaly.

   *backed by* `asmt-0006`

3. Two extensions are offered as conjecture and not tested here: that investor overreaction may explain Shiller's finding that long-term rates high relative to short rates tend to move down later, and Ohlson and Penman's suggestion that increased return volatility after stock splits may be linked to overreaction.

   *backed by* `asmt-0006`

4. Open question the paper does not answer: how the anomaly survives arbitrage, and more generally what the equilibrium conditions are for markets where some agents do not revise expectations by Bayes' rule. Russell and Thaler conclude that the existence of some rational agents is not sufficient to guarantee a rational expectations equilibrium in an economy containing quasi-rational agents. The authors state they do not have the space to address this and concentrate on the empirical test instead.

   *backed by* `asmt-0005`

### Related topics

`weak-form market efficiency`, `arbitrage`, `quasi-rational agents`

## 4. Testing overreaction on past returns: the De Bondt-Thaler research design

`create` · knowledge state **operational** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-004-r1` v2

**Slug** `testing-overreaction-on-past-returns-the-de-bondt-thaler-research-design`

### Summary

The procedure, in enough detail to reproduce or to attack. The design is the paper's contribution as much as the result: it conditions on past returns rather than on a corporate event, which is what makes a positive finding a weak-form violation.

### Assertions (9)

1. The design inverts the usual event study. Conventional semistrong tests form portfolios at t = 0 on an event affecting all stocks in the portfolio, such as an earnings announcement, then ask whether the later residual return is zero. This study asks instead whether systematic residual return behavior after formation is associated with systematic residual returns in the preformation months.

   *backed by* `asmt-0007`

2. Winner and loser portfolios are formed conditional upon past excess returns rather than on a firm-generated informational variable such as earnings, focusing on stocks with extreme capital gains or extreme losses over periods up to five years.

   *backed by* `asmt-0007`

3. The competing hypotheses as testable signs: market efficiency implies the expected residual return of both the winner and the loser portfolio, conditional on the information set at t-1, is zero. Overreaction implies the winner portfolio's expected residual is negative and the loser portfolio's is positive.

   *backed by* `asmt-0007`

4. Data: monthly returns for NYSE common stocks from the CRSP file at the University of Chicago, January 1926 to December 1982. An equally weighted arithmetic average rate of return on all CRSP-listed securities serves as the market index.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

5. Step 1: for every stock with at least 85 months of return data without missing values in between, starting January 1930, estimate the next 72 monthly residual returns; if data beyond month 85 are missing, calculate up to that point. Repeat 16 times, starting January 1930, January 1933, and so on to January 1975.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

6. Step 2: from December 1932, compute each stock's cumulative excess return over the prior 36 months, repeating for all nonoverlapping three-year periods to December 1977. Rank the cumulative excess returns low to high; the top 35 stocks (or top 50, or top decile) form the winner portfolio and the bottom 35 (or 50, or decile) the loser portfolio.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

7. Step 3: for both portfolios in each of the 16 periods, compute cumulative average residual returns over the next 36 months. If a security's return is missing in any month after formation, the stock is permanently dropped from that point and the CAR averages the available residual returns -- so every reported portfolio is implicitly rebalanced.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

8. Steps 4 and 5, the statistics: average the CARs across the 16 replications for each month t = 1 to 36. Overreaction predicts the winner average is negative, the loser average positive, and their difference positive. Significance for the difference uses a pooled estimate of the population variance; with two samples of equal size N the variance of the difference of sample means equals 2S²/N. A separate one-sample t-statistic tests whether an individual month's average residual return differs from zero.

   *backed by* `asmt-0010`

9. Limit on reading the statistics: t-statistics can be computed for each of the 36 postformation months, but they do not represent independent evidence.

   *backed by* `asmt-0010`

### Related topics

`CRSP`, `event study methodology`, `cumulative abnormal returns`

## 5. How returns were measured, and why the authors say it works against them

`create` · knowledge state **operational** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-005-r1` v2

**Slug** `how-returns-were-measured-and-why-the-authors-say-it-works-against-them`

### Summary

The residual-return choice behind every percentage the study reports, its stated advantage, and the parts of the defense that rest on an unpublished dissertation rather than on this paper.

### Assertions (5)

1. Three residual types were computed -- market-adjusted excess returns, market model residuals, and excess returns relative to the Sharpe-Lintner CAPM -- because a study of specific portfolios over periods as long as a decade cannot simply assume that model misspecification leaves the conclusions unchanged. All three are single-index models following from the CAPM, so the authors concede misspecification may still confound the results.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

2. Only market-adjusted excess returns are reported. The residual is the stock's return minus the market return, with no risk adjustment except for movements of the market as a whole, identical for all stocks. Because the same constant market return is subtracted from every stock's return in a period, the results are interpretable in raw dollar returns.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

3. The general defense of any equilibrium model: as long as the variation in the market's expected return is small relative to movements in the residual, the exact specification makes little difference to tests of market efficiency, because even the correct model would explain only a small part of the variation.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

4. The authors argue the market-adjusted choice biases the research design against the overreaction hypothesis, and report that winner and loser portfolios formed this way do not systematically differ in market value of equity, dividend yield or financial leverage. Both claims rest on De Bondt's separate unpublished dissertation, not on this paper, and cannot be checked from it.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

5. The same applies to the robustness claim: the authors state that results using market model and Sharpe-Lintner residuals are similar and insensitive to using December as the formation month, while stating in the same sentence that those results are not reported here. Treat this as asserted, not demonstrated.

   *backed by* `asmt-0014`

### Related topics

`market-adjusted excess returns`, `CAPM`, `model misspecification`

### Labels

- Three load-bearing claims rest on an unpublished 1985 dissertation not in this corpus: that the method biases against the hypothesis, that winner and loser portfolios do not differ on market value, dividend yield or leverage, and that other residual types give similar results. Each is unverifiable from the published paper.
- The omitted-risk-factor objection to the whole result turns on the second of those. If the portfolios did differ on some priced characteristic, the reversal could be risk compensation -- so the entry's least checkable claim is also its most load-bearing.
- 'Operational' is right: this is how to read every percentage in the corpus, not a finding in itself.

## 6. Known weaknesses of the De Bondt-Thaler design

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-006-r1` v2

**Slug** `known-weaknesses-of-the-de-bondt-thaler-design`

### Summary

The threats to the result, each stated by the authors with their reasoning attached. These are the questions a critic asks, and the paper already contains its answers -- which is not the same as the answers being right.

### Assertions (5)

1. Survivorship: because the study concentrates on companies with extraordinary returns, their attrition rate might deviate from normal. The authors judge the concern unjustified on the mechanism: when a security is delisted, suspended or halted, CRSP determines whether it can be traded at the last listed price; failing that it seeks a subsequent quote; and if no quote exists because shareholders receive nothing, the return is entered as minus one.

   *backed by* `asmt-0011`

2. Selection: requiring 85 subsequent returns biases the sample towards large, established firms. The authors argue this strengthens rather than weakens the result, since it counters the critique that overreaction is mostly a small-firm phenomenon. Between 347 and 1,089 NYSE stocks participate in the various replications.

   *backed by* `asmt-0011`

3. Data frequency: monthly rather than daily data were chosen to avoid measurement problems that arise with daily data for both risk and return variables, including the bid-ask effect and the consequences of infrequent trading.

   *backed by* `asmt-0011`

4. Window length: the 36-month test period is a compromise between an adequate number of independent replications and a period long enough to matter for asset pricing. It also matches Benjamin Graham's contention that the interval required for a substantial underevaluation to correct itself averages approximately one and a half to two and a half years. Selected experiments use one-, two- and five-year periods, and the number of independent replications varies inversely with formation length.

   *backed by* `asmt-0011`

5. Formation month: choosing December, and therefore January as the starting month, is described as essentially arbitrary. Some tests use May as the formation month to check whether the choice affects the results.

   *backed by* `asmt-0011`

### Related topics

`survivorship bias`, `selection bias`, `research design`

### Labels

- Attribute the selection-bias argument to the authors rather than stating it as a property of the result.
- Note that the May robustness check is reported without a result.

## 7. The loser portfolios are less risky, not more

`create` · knowledge state **preliminary** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-007-r1` v2

**Slug** `the-loser-portfolios-are-less-risky-not-more`

### Summary

The result that makes the reversal hard to dismiss as compensation for risk, and the measurement bias it implies.

### Assertions (4)

1. For all the experiments in Table I, the average CAPM betas of the securities in the winner portfolios are significantly larger than those of the loser portfolios. For the three-year experiment the betas are 1.369 and 1.026 respectively, with a t-statistic on the difference of 3.09. The betas are estimated from the market model over the 60 months prior to portfolio formation.

   *backed by* `asmt-0015`

2. If the CAPM is correct, the loser portfolios therefore not only outperform the winner portfolios but are also significantly less risky, so the reported results are likely to underestimate both the true magnitude and the statistical significance of the overreaction effect.

   *backed by* `asmt-0015`

3. The bias is worst for the winner portfolio: the residual calculations assume its CAPM beta equals 1.00 rather than 1.369.

   *backed by* `asmt-0015`

4. The authors suggest this systematic bias may be responsible for the observed asymmetry between the portfolios. That is offered as a possibility, not a demonstration.

   *backed by* `asmt-0015`

### Related topics

`CAPM`, `beta`, `risk-adjusted returns`

### Labels

- 'Less risky' is conditional on the CAPM being correct, and the same corpus argues that single-index models may be misspecified. The condition is present but reads as a formality; it is the substance of the claim.
- Beta is estimated over the 60 months prior to formation -- the same window that defines the portfolios. A portfolio selected on extreme past returns may have unstable betas across the formation boundary, which would affect the comparison. Neither the paper nor the entry addresses this.
- 'Preliminary' is right for a single-study measurement.
- The conjecture that the beta bias explains the asymmetry is correctly marked as the authors' possibility and must not harden into an explanation.

## 8. The January effect in loser portfolios, unexplained

`create` · knowledge state **preliminary** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-008-r1` v2

**Slug** `the-january-effect-in-loser-portfolios-unexplained`

### Summary

The corpus's most striking result and the one the authors say they cannot account for. Its persistence over five successive Januaries is what breaks the standard tax-loss selling explanation.

### Assertions (5)

1. Most of the loser portfolio's excess returns are realized in January. In months t = 1, t = 13 and t = 25 the loser portfolio earns excess returns of 8.1 per cent (t = 3.21), 5.6 per cent (t = 3.07) and 4.0 per cent (t = 2.76).

   *backed by* `asmt-0016`

2. In a five-year experiment the January effect on the loser portfolio is observed as late as five Januaries after portfolio formation.

   *backed by* `asmt-0016`

3. The loser portfolio also tends to decline in value relative to the market between October and December, which agrees with the naive version of the tax-loss selling hypothesis, while the winner portfolio gains value at year end and loses some in January.

   *backed by* `asmt-0016`

4. ## 10. What the winner-loser reversal implies for the small firm and January anomalies

`create` · knowledge state **contested** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-010-r1` v2

**Slug** `what-the-winner-loser-reversal-implies-for-the-small-firm-and-january-anomalies`

### Summary

How this study's results bear on three neighbouring anomalies, and where the corpus disagrees with itself and with others. There is no settled account here and this entry does not supply one. The background dispute over the price-earnings anomaly belongs in its own entry; what remains here is what this paper's results imply.

### Assertions (6)

1. This study's results support Basu's price-ratio hypothesis -- high P/E stocks overvalued, low P/E stocks undervalued -- but the authors note the argument implies the P/E effect is also mostly a January phenomenon, and that no evidence supports that claim except the persistent positive relationship between dividend yield, which correlates with the P/E ratio, and January excess returns.

   *backed by* `asmt-0018`

2. On the small firm effect: work by Blume and Stambaugh, Keim and Reinganum largely redefines it as a losing-firm effect around the turn of the year, and this study lends further credence to that view, since losers persistently earn exceptionally large January returns while winners do not.

   *backed by* `asmt-0018`

3. Cutting against that reading, from the same study: the companies in the extreme portfolios do not systematically differ with respect to market capitalization. The corpus contains both findings and reconciles neither.

   *backed by* `asmt-0018`

4. The results raise two unanswered questions for the tax-loss selling explanation of the January effect. If selling pressure disappears in early January and prices rebound to equilibrium, why does the loser portfolio rebound again in the second January of the test period, and the third and fourth? And why is the January effect so much larger in magnitude than the selling pressure that supposedly caused it in the final months of the previous year? The authors offer two possible answers: investors may wait years before realizing losses, and the market as a whole shows seasonality.

   *backed by* `asmt-0018`

5. A live disagreement: Reinganum finds a considerably smaller January seasonal effect related to company size even after purging the data of tax-loss selling effects. The authors dispute this on the ground that his tax-loss measure uses only the six months of relative price movement before formation, so if many investors wait longer than six months to realize losses, the small-firm portfolio may still contain many losers.

   *backed by* `asmt-0018`

6. Background, filed separately: the price-earnings anomaly is Basu's observation that lowest-decile P/E stocks earn larger risk-adjusted returns than high P/E stocks, and three incompatible explanations of it are in play -- CAPM misspecification with an unidentified omitted factor (which the authors object is untestable), Reinganum's claim that the small firm effect subsumes it (contradicted by Basu controlling for size and by Graham finding the effect within the Dow thirty), and the behavioral price-ratio hypothesis.

   *backed by* `asmt-0004`

## 11. Excess volatility as evidence for overreaction: disputed

**Slug** `excess-volatility-as-evidence-for-overreaction-disputed`

### Summary

The other anomaly the overreaction hypothesis is offered to explain, kept separate because the evidence is contested and the corpus contains both sides.

### Assertions (4)

1. Shiller concludes that, at least over the last century, dividends simply do not vary enough to rationally justify observed aggregate price movements.
