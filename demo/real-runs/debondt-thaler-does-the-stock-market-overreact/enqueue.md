## 1. NYSE 1926-1982: extreme prior-loser portfolios outperformed prior winners by about 25 per cent over the following three years

`create` · knowledge state **supported** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-001-r1` v2

**Slug** `nyse-1926-1982-extreme-prior-loser-portfolios-outperformed-prior-winners-by-abou`

### Summary

Over sixteen non-overlapping three-year periods of NYSE data from 1926 to 1982, portfolios of the 35 stocks with the most extreme prior three-year losses beat the market by 19.6 per cent in the following 36 months while extreme prior winners trailed it by 5.0 per cent -- a 24.6 per cent spread, t = 2.20. The spread widens as the formation period lengthens and vanishes entirely at a one-year formation period. Most of it is realised in January, not evenly across the three years. The losers also carried lower CAPM betas than the winners (1.026 against 1.369), so they outperformed while being less risky on the model's own terms. These are market-adjusted residual returns rather than the returns of an implementable strategy: the paper does not measure whether the spread survives transaction costs. De Bondt and Thaler read the result as market overreaction and as a violation of weak-form efficiency. It rests on one paper with no independent replication in this corpus.

### Assertions (8)

1. Portfolios of extreme prior losers outperformed extreme prior winners by about 24.6 per cent over the 36 months after formation (t = 2.20), averaged over sixteen non-overlapping three-year periods of NYSE data, 1926-1982.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The excess return is concentrated in January rather than spread across the test period; see the separate entry on the unexplained January concentration.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The effect scales with the extremeness of the formation period, and no reversal is observed at all for a one-year formation period.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Winner portfolios carried significantly higher CAPM betas than loser portfolios (1.369 against 1.026, t = 3.09), so the losers outperformed while being less risky on the CAPM's terms.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. Because the residual calculation assumes a beta of one for both portfolios, the reported magnitude and significance are likely understated rather than overstated.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The reversal appears in the second and third years after formation; at twelve months the spread is 5.4 per cent and not significant (t = 0.77).

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. Returns are market-adjusted residuals, not net-of-cost strategy returns; the source does not measure whether the spread survives transaction costs.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

8. The result rests on one paper and one independence group, not on independent replication.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 2. Testing overreaction by forming portfolios on past returns rather than on an event

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-002-r1` v2

**Slug** `testing-overreaction-by-forming-portfolios-on-past-returns-rather-than-on-an-eve`

### Summary

The design conditions portfolio formation on past excess returns rather than on a firm-generated event such as an earnings announcement, which is what makes it a weak-form test. Monthly CRSP returns for NYSE common stocks from January 1926 to December 1982 are used, with an equally weighted index; stocks need 85 consecutive months of data, which biases toward large established firms and thereby answers the small-firm critique in advance. Three residual types were computed and gave similar results, so only market-adjusted excess returns are reported -- a choice the authors argue biases the test against their own hypothesis.

### Assertions (6)

1. Portfolios are formed conditional on past excess returns rather than on a firm-generated informational event, which is what makes this a weak-form rather than a semistrong-form test.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The sample is CRSP monthly returns for NYSE common stocks, January 1926 to December 1982, with an equally weighted arithmetic average of all CRSP-listed securities as the market index.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The 85-month data requirement biases selection toward large established firms, which the authors treat as strengthening the result against the small-firm objection; between 347 and 1,089 stocks participate per replication.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Market-adjusted, market-model and Sharpe-Lintner CAPM residuals gave similar results; only market-adjusted excess returns are reported, and all three are single-index models so misspecification could still confound the results.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. Survivorship bias is addressed through CRSP's delisting handling, with a return of minus one entered when shareholders receive nothing; a stock that drops out is removed permanently, giving an implicit rebalancing.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. December was chosen as the formation month essentially arbitrarily, and some tests use May to check that the seasonal result is not an artifact of that choice.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

### Related topics

`research design`, `CRSP`, `survivorship bias`, `weak-form efficiency`

### Labels

- The claim that market-adjusted excess returns bias the design against the overreaction hypothesis is the authors' own derivation, attributed in the paper to De Bondt's unpublished dissertation. The entry states it as a property of the method; it should be read as the authors' argument about their own design.
- The assertion that all three residual types give similar results is reported without the underlying numbers, because the paper does not publish them -- only the market-adjusted results are shown. The claim is therefore unverifiable from the source as presented.
- The design defences are internally consistent and each names its direction of bias, which is unusually explicit; no correction needed.

## 3. Unexplained: loser-portfolio January excess returns persisting five years after formation

`create` · knowledge state **preliminary** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-003-r1` v2

**Slug** `unexplained-loser-portfolio-january-excess-returns-persisting-five-years-after-f`

### Summary

Most of the overreaction effect is realised in January -- the loser portfolio earns 8.1 per cent in month 1, 5.6 per cent in month 13 and 4.0 per cent in month 25 -- and in a five-year experiment the January effect is still visible in the fifth January after formation. The authors state plainly that they cannot explain this. It also poses two problems for the standard tax-loss-selling account: why the loser portfolio rebounds again in the second, third and fourth Januaries, and why the rebound is so much larger than the selling pressure said to cause it.

### Assertions (5)

1. Loser-portfolio excess returns are concentrated in January: 8.1 per cent in month 1 (t = 3.21), 5.6 per cent in month 13 (t = 3.07), 4.0 per cent in month 25 (t = 2.76).

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. In a five-year test with 46 yearly replications the January effect on losers is observed as late as the fifth January after portfolio formation. The replications are not independent, so no statistical test is performed on this experiment.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The authors record the January concentration as remaining without adequate explanation.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. The finding raises two unanswered questions for tax-loss selling: why the rebound recurs in later Januaries, and why it exceeds in magnitude the selling pressure said to have caused it.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. The results support redefining the small firm effect as a losing-firm effect around the turn of the year, though the extreme portfolios do not systematically differ in market capitalization.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

### Related topics

`January effect`, `tax-loss selling`, `small firm effect`, `seasonality`

### Labels

- The small-firm assertion is a conclusion about a neighbouring literature rather than part of the January puzzle, and it also appears in the assessment's qualifying set. It may belong in its own entry rather than inside an open-question record.
- The five-year persistence is correctly marked as untested -- the 46 yearly replications overlap, so no significance can be attached. Retaining that caveat is what keeps this entry honest, and it should not be dropped if the entry is later summarised.
- 'Preliminary' is the correct knowledge state: the authors describe the January concentration as remaining without adequate explanation, so this records an open question rather than a finding.
- One paper, one independence group. The tax-loss questions are the authors' challenges to an incumbent explanation, not established defects in it.
