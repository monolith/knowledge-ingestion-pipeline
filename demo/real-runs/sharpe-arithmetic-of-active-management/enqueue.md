## 1. The arithmetic of active management (Sharpe, 1991)

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-001-r1` v2

**Slug** `the-arithmetic-of-active-management-sharpe-1991`

### Summary

Sharpe's 1991 result: relative to any named market, the average actively managed dollar earns the market return before costs and less than the average passively managed dollar after costs. Steps one and two are arithmetic and hold for any period; only the premise that active management costs more is empirical, and it is the one place the result could fail. This is the originating paper's own statement of the result -- one source, one argument, not replicated evidence.

### Assertions (6)

1. Before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar equals the return on the average passively managed dollar. After costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar is lower. Both hold for any time period and use only addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The market return over any period is the weighted average of the returns on the securities in that market, using beginning-of-period market values as weights. A passive holder therefore earns precisely the market return before costs.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. Assertion 1 follows because the market return is also a weighted average of the returns on the market's passive and active segments: if the market return and the passive return are equal, the active return must equal them too.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Assertion 2 rests on one empirical premise, not on arithmetic: actively managing a given number of dollars costs more than managing them passively, because active managers pay for more research and more trading -- security analysts, brokers, traders, specialists and other market-makers must all be paid. Equal before-cost returns plus higher active costs give a lower after-cost active return.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. This is the paper's answer to four claims commonly made against passive management: that index-fund outperformance may prove transitory, that active management is better in small stocks, that the case for passive rests on unrealistic equilibrium theories, and that a good business-school graduate should beat an index fund over a market cycle. The paper does not test these claims; it holds that they require the laws of arithmetic to be suspended.

   *backed by* `asmt-0003`

6. The support is one independence group -- a single paper making a single argument. The five statements above are steps in that argument and must not be read as five independent confirmations. The arithmetic steps do not need replication; the cost premise does.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`, `asmt-0003`

`passive management`, `index funds`, `investment costs`, `performance measurement`

## 2. Active and passive management: the definitions the arithmetic requires

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-002-r1` v2

**Slug** `active-and-passive-management-the-definitions-the-arithmetic-requires`

### Summary

The three definitions Sharpe's result is stated in terms of. They are load-bearing: the proof does not close without all three, and a fund can only be checked against the result by applying them literally.

### Assertions (4)

1. A market must be named before any investor is classified -- the stocks in the S&P 500, or a set of 'small' stocks. Every investor holding securities from that market is then classified as active or passive. Stated without a named market, the arithmetic has no content.

   *backed by* `asmt-0002`

2. A passive investor always holds every security in the chosen market, each in the same proportion as the market itself: if security X is 3 per cent of the market's value, 3 per cent of the passive portfolio is invested in X. Equivalently, a passive manager holds the same percentage of the total outstanding amount of every security in the market.

   *backed by* `asmt-0002`

3. An active investor is anyone who is not passive; an active portfolio differs from the passive one at some or all times. Because active managers act on perceptions of mispricing and those perceptions change relatively often, they trade frequently -- which is where the term comes from.

   *backed by* `asmt-0002`

4. The two categories are complements, so they exhaust the market's holders without remainder. That exhaustiveness is what allows the market return to be decomposed into exactly two segments.

   *backed by* `asmt-0002`

### Related topics

`market definition`, `index funds`, `portfolio construction`

### Labels

- These are one paper's stipulated definitions, not consensus industry usage. Real index funds routinely fail the passive definition, which the paper itself notes -- so the definition is a standard to measure against, not a description of practice.
- The exhaustiveness assertion is doing real work and is correctly separated from the definitions themselves: it is what licenses the two-segment decomposition in the proof.
- Content is carried, not described. A reader can classify a fund from this entry alone.

## 3. Measurement failures that make active management look better than it is

`create` · knowledge state **operational** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-003-r1` v2

**Slug** `measurement-failures-that-make-active-management-look-better-than-it-is`

### Summary

Sharpe's three reasons why published comparisons can show active managers beating passive ones despite the arithmetic. Each is a defect in how the comparison was built, checkable against a specific study; none is evidence against the result. The paper does not present the three as exhaustive.

### Assertions (6)

1. Reason one: the comparator treated as passive may not be passive by the definition. Some index-fund managers sample the market instead of holding all its securities in market proportions, and some charge fees high enough to bring total costs to equal or exceed those of active managers.

   *backed by* `asmt-0004`

2. Reason two: the set of active managers studied may exclude part of the market's non-passive component -- for example when only professional or institutional managers are counted and individual investors are left out. The average institutionally managed dollar could beat the average passive dollar after costs, but only if individual investors pay the institutions' added active costs through inferior performance.

   *backed by* `asmt-0005`

3. A second form of reason two: the active managers hold securities from outside the compared market. Equity mutual funds holding cash are often compared with an all-equity index; such funds are generally beaten badly in up markets and sometimes exceed the index in down markets, which is an artifact of the mismatch rather than evidence about skill.

   *backed by* `asmt-0005`

4. A third form of reason two -- survivorship bias: excluding managers who went out of business during the period removes those likely to have had especially poor returns, so measured results come out better than those actually obtained by the average actively managed dollar.

   *backed by* `asmt-0005`

5. Reason three, which the paper calls possibly the most important in practice: the summary statistic may not represent the average actively managed dollar. To compute that average, weight each manager's return by the dollars under management at the beginning of the period. Comparisons that use a simple average across all managers, large and small, or the median active manager's return, do not measure it.

   *backed by* `asmt-0006`

6. Equal weighting imports a small-capitalization bias, because equity fund managers with less money under management tend to favour stocks with smaller outstanding values. The measured 'average active manager' is therefore beaten badly when small-cap stocks underperform large-caps and may exceed the market when they do well. In both cases the average actively managed dollar still underperforms the market net of costs, so a measured swing in either direction is consistent with the arithmetic holding.

   *backed by* `asmt-0006`

### Related topics

`survivorship bias`, `benchmark mismatch`, `capitalization weighting`, `index funds`

### Labels

- The summary states the three reasons are not exhaustive, but the source's named examples of the others -- differential treatment of dividend reinvestment, mergers and acquisitions -- appear nowhere. A reader cannot tell what else to look for.
- The small-cap bias is presented as a persistent empirical regularity. The source hedges it: results of this kind of comparison are in principle unpredictable, and only certain regularities persist. That hedge is absent.
- One source. The three failures are one author's taxonomy, not three independently observed phenomena, and the entry should not read as though the field has converged on this list.
- The institutional-versus-individual point is correctly kept as a conditional. The source concedes institutions could beat passive dollars if individuals pay for it; flattening that into a denial would misstate the paper.

## 4. What the arithmetic does not say about individual managers

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-004-r1` v2

**Slug** `what-the-arithmetic-does-not-say-about-individual-managers`

### Summary

The scope limits Sharpe states for his result. It constrains a dollar-weighted average, not every manager, and the paper says explicitly what remains possible -- along with the share condition that still binds in each case. One source, one argument.

### Assertions (3)

1. Some active managers can beat their passive counterparts even after costs. Those who do must collectively manage a minority share of the actively managed dollars in the market in question.

   *backed by* `asmt-0007`

2. An investor such as a pension fund can choose a set of active managers that collectively returns more than a passive alternative after costs. Not every manager in the set needs to beat their passive counterpart -- only those managing a majority of the investor's actively managed funds.

   *backed by* `asmt-0007`

3. Reading, not the source's wording: the result is therefore not the claim that active management cannot work. It is a constraint on an average, and the minority and majority conditions above are what make it one.

   *backed by* `asmt-0007`

## 5. Measuring a manager: benchmark, not peer group

`create` · knowledge state **operational** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-005-r1` v2

**Slug** `measuring-a-manager-benchmark-not-peer-group`

### Summary

The corollary Sharpe draws for practice. Peer-group comparison is ruled out and two apparent substitutes are rejected; what replaces them is a passive comparator fixed before the measurement period.

### Assertions (4)

1. Peer-group comparisons are dangerous and should not be used for decision-making. The capitalization-weighted average performance of active managers is necessarily inferior to a passive alternative, which makes it a poor yardstick, and most peer-group averages are not capitalization-weighted, which adds further biases.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

2. Investing equal amounts with many managers is not a practical alternative, and investing with the median manager is less practical still, since that manager's identity is not known in advance.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

3. Measure a manager's return against a comparable passive alternative -- a benchmark or 'normal portfolio'. It must be a feasible alternative and it must be identified in advance of the period over which performance is measured.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`

4. Only under that kind of measurement can an active manager, or someone who hires active managers, know whether they are in the minority who have beaten viable passive alternatives.

   *backed by* `asmt-0008`, `asmt-0007`

### Related topics

`benchmark`, `normal portfolio`, `performance measurement`, `peer group comparison`

### Labels

- The entry requires a feasible passive benchmark identified in advance but gives no method for constructing one. The source does not supply one either. A reader could follow this rule and still choose a benchmark badly; the entry should not read as though naming the requirement solves it.
- The prohibition on peer-group comparison is stated with its reason -- necessarily inferior average, usually not capitalization-weighted -- so a reader can tell when it applies rather than following it blindly.
- This is one author's prescription from 1991, not a standard. Presented as operational guidance it should carry that attribution.

## 6. Scope conditions of the arithmetic of active management

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-006-r1` v2

**Slug** `scope-conditions-of-the-arithmetic-of-active-management`

### Summary

The simplifications the proof runs under, stated by the author in footnotes. Two are declared not to affect the principle; the third bounds how literally the before-costs equality applies to a real fund.

### Assertions (4)

1. When computing an investor's share of a security's outstanding amount, cross-holdings within the market should be netted out.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

2. Events during the period -- mergers, new listings, reinvestment of dividends -- require more complex calculations. The paper ignores them for simplicity and states they do not affect the basic principles.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

3. The claim that a passive manager earns precisely the market return assumes passive managers buy before the period begins and do not sell until after it ends. When passive managers do trade within the period they may have to trade with active managers, who supply the desired liquidity at a price.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`

4. The before-costs equality is therefore a limiting case rather than a description of any real index fund's period.

   *backed by* `asmt-0009`, `asmt-0001`

### Related topics

`cross-holdings`, `liquidity`, `scope conditions`
