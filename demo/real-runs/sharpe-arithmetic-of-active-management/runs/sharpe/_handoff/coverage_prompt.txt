## The arithmetic of active management (Sharpe, 1991)
- Before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar equals the return on the average passively managed dollar. After costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar is lower. Both hold for any time period and use only addition, subtraction, multiplication and division.
- The market return over any period is the weighted average of the returns on the securities in that market, using beginning-of-period market values as weights. A passive holder therefore earns precisely the market return before costs.
- Assertion 1 follows because the market return is also a weighted average of the returns on the market's passive and active segments: if the market return and the passive return are equal, the active return must equal them too.
- Assertion 2 rests on one empirical premise, not on arithmetic: actively managing a given number of dollars costs more than managing them passively, because active managers pay for more research and more trading -- security analysts, brokers, traders, specialists and other market-makers must all be paid. Equal before-cost returns plus higher active costs give a lower after-cost active return.
- This is the paper's answer to four claims commonly made against passive management: that index-fund outperformance may prove transitory, that active management is better in small stocks, that the case for passive rests on unrealistic equilibrium theories, and that a good business-school graduate should beat an index fund over a market cycle. The paper does not test these claims; it holds that they require the laws of arithmetic to be suspended.
- The support is one independence group -- a single paper making a single argument. The five statements above are steps in that argument and must not be read as five independent confirmations. The arithmetic steps do not need replication; the cost premise does.
## Active and passive management: the definitions the arithmetic requires
- A market must be named before any investor is classified -- the stocks in the S&P 500, or a set of 'small' stocks. Every investor holding securities from that market is then classified as active or passive. Stated without a named market, the arithmetic has no content.
- A passive investor always holds every security in the chosen market, each in the same proportion as the market itself: if security X is 3 per cent of the market's value, 3 per cent of the passive portfolio is invested in X. Equivalently, a passive manager holds the same percentage of the total outstanding amount of every security in the market.
- An active investor is anyone who is not passive; an active portfolio differs from the passive one at some or all times. Because active managers act on perceptions of mispricing and those perceptions change relatively often, they trade frequently -- which is where the term comes from.
- The two categories are complements, so they exhaust the market's holders without remainder. That exhaustiveness is what allows the market return to be decomposed into exactly two segments.
## Measurement failures that make active management look better than it is
- Reason one: the comparator treated as passive may not be passive by the definition. Some index-fund managers sample the market instead of holding all its securities in market proportions, and some charge fees high enough to bring total costs to equal or exceed those of active managers.
- Reason two: the set of active managers studied may exclude part of the market's non-passive component -- for example when only professional or institutional managers are counted and individual investors are left out. The average institutionally managed dollar could beat the average passive dollar after costs, but only if individual investors pay the institutions' added active costs through inferior performance.
- A second form of reason two: the active managers hold securities from outside the compared market. Equity mutual funds holding cash are often compared with an all-equity index; such funds are generally beaten badly in up markets and sometimes exceed the index in down markets, which is an artifact of the mismatch rather than evidence about skill.
- A third form of reason two -- survivorship bias: excluding managers who went out of business during the period removes those likely to have had especially poor returns, so measured results come out better than those actually obtained by the average actively managed dollar.
- Reason three, which the paper calls possibly the most important in practice: the summary statistic may not represent the average actively managed dollar. To compute that average, weight each manager's return by the dollars under management at the beginning of the period. Comparisons that use a simple average across all managers, large and small, or the median active manager's return, do not measure it.
- Equal weighting imports a small-capitalization bias, because equity fund managers with less money under management tend to favour stocks with smaller outstanding values. The measured 'average active manager' is therefore beaten badly when small-cap stocks underperform large-caps and may exceed the market when they do well. In both cases the average actively managed dollar still underperforms the market net of costs, so a measured swing in either direction is consistent with the arithmetic holding.
## What the arithmetic does not say about individual managers
- Some active managers can beat their passive counterparts even after costs. Those who do must collectively manage a minority share of the actively managed dollars in the market in question.
- An investor such as a pension fund can choose a set of active managers that collectively returns more than a passive alternative after costs. Not every manager in the set needs to beat their passive counterpart -- only those managing a majority of the investor's actively managed funds.
- Reading, not the source's wording: the result is therefore not the claim that active management cannot work. It is a constraint on an average, and the minority and majority conditions above are what make it one.
## Measuring a manager: benchmark, not peer group
- Peer-group comparisons are dangerous and should not be used for decision-making. The capitalization-weighted average performance of active managers is necessarily inferior to a passive alternative, which makes it a poor yardstick, and most peer-group averages are not capitalization-weighted, which adds further biases.
- Investing equal amounts with many managers is not a practical alternative, and investing with the median manager is less practical still, since that manager's identity is not known in advance.
- Measure a manager's return against a comparable passive alternative -- a benchmark or 'normal portfolio'. It must be a feasible alternative and it must be identified in advance of the period over which performance is measured.
- Only under that kind of measurement can an active manager, or someone who hires active managers, know whether they are in the minority who have beaten viable passive alternatives.
## Scope conditions of the arithmetic of active management
- When computing an investor's share of a security's outstanding amount, cross-holdings within the market should be netted out.
- Events during the period -- mergers, new listings, reinvestment of dividends -- require more complex calculations. The paper ignores them for simplicity and states they do not affect the basic principles.
- The claim that a passive manager earns precisely the market return assumes passive managers buy before the period begins and do not sell until after it ends. When passive managers do trade within the period they may have to trade with active managers, who supply the desired liquidity at a price.
- The before-costs equality is therefore a limiting case rather than a description of any real index fund's period.
