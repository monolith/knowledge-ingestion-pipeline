## 1. Arithmetic of active management: within a defined market, the average actively managed dollar underperforms the average passive dollar after costs

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-001-r1` v2

**Slug** `arithmetic-of-active-management-the-average-active-dollar-cannot-beat-the-averag`

### Summary

Within a named market, if every holder is classified as either passive (holding every security in market proportions) or active (anyone else), then the market return is the value-weighted average of its securities, a passive holder earns exactly that return before costs, and the active remainder must therefore earn it too. Adding the premise that active management costs more -- research and trading -- the after-cost average active return must be lower. The first half is arithmetic and is not contingent on market conditions; the second half depends on the cost premise, which the source asserts rather than measures. The result constrains the dollar-weighted average only: a minority of active managers can beat passive alternatives after costs. It further assumes passive managers do not trade within the measurement period, and it rests on one paper and one deductive chain rather than on corroborated evidence.

### Assertions (7)

1. Before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar equals the return on the average passively managed dollar within any defined market.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The market return is the weighted average of the returns on the market's securities using beginning market values as weights, so a passive holder earns precisely it before costs.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. After costs the average actively managed dollar returns less. ASSERTED, NOT MEASURED: this depends on the premise that active management costs more than passive, which the source states as fact and does not quantify.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. The result holds only relative to a named market and only because active is defined as the complement of passive, making the two exhaustive. Stated without a market it has no content.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. The constraint is on the dollar-weighted average, not on individual managers. A minority of active managers can beat passive alternatives after costs, and an allocator can hold a set that collectively does.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The before-costs equality assumes passive managers buy before the period and do not sell until after it; when they trade within the period they may trade with active managers supplying liquidity at a price.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. The result rests on a single source and one deductive chain, not on independent replication.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 2. Why measured active-manager performance appears to contradict the arithmetic, and how to benchmark instead

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-002-r1` v2

**Slug** `why-measured-active-manager-performance-appears-to-contradict-the-arithmetic-and`

### Summary

Sharpe attributes every empirical result showing active managers beating the market to one of three measurement failures, and prescribes a replacement. The nominally passive managers may sample rather than replicate, or charge fees rivalling active ones. The active set may omit part of the non-passive component -- individual investors, funds holding assets outside the compared market, or managers who went out of business, producing survivorship bias. And the summary statistic may be an equal- or median-weighted average rather than a dollar-weighted one, which imports a small-capitalization bias. The prescription is to measure a manager against a feasible passive benchmark identified in advance, not against a peer group.

### Assertions (7)

1. Empirical analyses that appear to refute the arithmetic are guilty of improper measurement rather than presenting counterevidence.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. Nominally passive managers may sample the market rather than hold all securities in market proportions, or charge fees bringing their costs to equal or exceed active managers'.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. Studies restricted to professional or institutional active managers exclude other active holders such as individual investors; for institutions to beat passive after costs, individuals must absorb the difference through inferior performance.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Excluding active managers who went out of business produces survivorship bias, making measured results better than the average actively managed dollar achieved.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. Equally weighted averages of active manager returns carry a small-capitalization bias, because managers with less money favour smaller stocks; this moves the measured average in either direction while the dollar-weighted average still underperforms net of costs.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. Peer-group comparisons are a poor basis for decisions, and most peer-group averages are not capitalization-weighted, adding further bias.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. A manager should be measured against a comparable passive alternative -- a benchmark or normal portfolio -- that is feasible and identified in advance of the period.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`
