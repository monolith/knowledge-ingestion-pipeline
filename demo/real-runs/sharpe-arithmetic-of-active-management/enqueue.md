## 1. Arithmetic of active management: the result and its two proofs

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-001-r1` v2

**Slug** `arithmetic-of-active-management-the-result-and-its-two-proofs`

### Summary

Sharpe's two assertions, the definitions they rest on, and the two proofs -- the first from the weighted-average identity, the second from the premise that active management costs more.

### Assertions (15)

1. Sharpe opens by quoting claims investment professionals make against passive management -- that index-fund outperformance may be transitory, that active management is better in small stocks, that the case for passive rests on unrealistic equilibrium theories, and that any good business-school graduate should beat an index fund over a market cycle. He treats these not as hypotheses to test but as claims that can only be justified by assuming the laws of arithmetic have been suspended for the convenience of those who choose careers as active managers.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. Sharpe's two assertions are that, given sensible definitions of 'active' and 'passive' management, (1) before costs the return on the average actively managed dollar equals the return on the average passively managed dollar, and (2) after costs it is less. Both hold for any time period and depend only on addition, subtraction, multiplication and division -- so they are accounting identities rather than empirical regularities that better data could overturn.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The result is defined relative to a chosen market, not to investing in general: a market must be selected first -- the stocks in the S&P 500, or a set of 'small' stocks -- and then every investor holding securities from that market is classified as either active or passive. The arithmetic therefore says nothing until the market is named, and it holds separately within each market so chosen.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. A passive investor is defined as one who always holds every security from the chosen market, each represented in the same proportion as in the market: if security X is 3 per cent of the market's value, 3 per cent of the passive portfolio is invested in X. Equivalently, a passive manager holds the same percentage of the total outstanding amount of every security in the market.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. An active investor is defined negatively as anyone who is not passive, so an active portfolio differs from the passive one at some or all times. Active managers trade frequently because they act on perceptions of mispricing and those misperceptions change relatively often -- which is where the term 'active' comes from. Because the definition is the complement of passive, the two categories exhaust the market's holders without remainder.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The market return over any specified period is the weighted average of the returns on the securities in that market, using beginning market values as weights, and because a passive investor holds exactly those securities in exactly those proportions, each passive manager obtains precisely the market return before costs.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. Assertion 1 follows because the market return must itself equal a weighted average of the returns on the passive and active segments of the market: if the market return and the passive return are the same, the active return must be too. The average actively managed dollar therefore earns exactly the market return before costs, established by arithmetic alone rather than by evidence.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

8. Assertion 2 rests on one factual premise rather than on arithmetic alone: that actively managing a given number of dollars costs more than managing them passively, because active managers must pay for more research and pay more for trading -- security analysts, brokers, traders, specialists and other market-makers all have to be paid. Given equal before-cost returns and greater active costs, the after-cost return from active management must be lower.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

9. Sharpe identifies three reasons why presented evidence can appear to show active managers beating the market or passive managers, despite the arithmetic: the nominally passive managers may not be truly passive, the set of active managers may not represent the whole non-passive component of the market, and the summary statistic used may not represent the average actively managed dollar. All three are measurement failures rather than counterexamples.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

10. The result does not imply that no active manager can win. It is perfectly possible for some active managers to beat their passive counterparts even after costs -- but those who do must collectively manage a minority share of the actively managed dollars in the market in question, because the dollar-weighted average cannot exceed the passive return.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

11. An investor such as a pension fund can choose a set of active managers that collectively returns more than a passive alternative even after costs. Not every manager in the set needs to beat their passive counterpart -- only those managing a majority of the investor's actively managed funds.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

12. Peer-group comparisons are a poor basis for investment decisions. Because the capitalization-weighted average performance of active managers is necessarily inferior to a passive alternative, that average is a bad yardstick -- and because most peer-group averages are not capitalization-weighted, they carry additional biases on top.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

13. The arithmetic carries scope conditions set out in the footnotes. Cross-holdings within the market should be netted out when computing each investor's share of a security's outstanding amount, and events during the period such as mergers, new listings and reinvestment of dividends require more complex calculations which the paper deliberately ignores while stating that they do not affect the basic principles.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

14. The claim that a passive manager earns precisely the market return assumes passive managers buy their securities before the period begins and do not sell until after it ends. When passive managers do trade within the period they may have to trade with active managers, who supply the desired liquidity at a price -- so the strict result is a limiting case rather than a description of any real index fund's period.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

15. The second reason is that the studied set of active managers may exclude part of the market's non-passive component -- for instance individual investors, when an analysis considers only professional or institutional active managers. Sharpe notes it is possible for the average institutionally managed dollar to beat the average passive dollar after costs, but only if non-institutional individual investors are foolish enough to pay the institutions' added active costs through inferior performance.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 2. Why measured active-manager performance appears to contradict the arithmetic

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-002-r1` v2

**Slug** `why-measured-active-manager-performance-appears-to-contradict-the-arithmetic`

### Summary

The three reasons Sharpe gives for empirical results that appear to show active managers winning: index funds that sample rather than replicate, active sets that omit part of the non-passive component, and averages weighted by manager rather than by dollar.

### Assertions (5)

1. The first reason apparent counterevidence arises is that the managers treated as passive may not meet the strict definition: some index-fund managers sample the market rather than holding all its securities in market proportions, and some charge fees high enough to bring their total costs to equal or exceed those of active managers.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. Survivorship bias is a third instance of the unrepresentative-active-set problem: when the set of active managers excludes those who went out of business during the period, and such managers are likely to have had especially poor returns, the measured results are better than those actually obtained by the average actively managed dollar.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The third and, Sharpe says, possibly most important reason in practice is that the summary statistic used may not represent the average actively managed dollar. To compute that average correctly each manager's return must be weighted by the dollars under management at the beginning of the period, whereas comparisons often use a simple average across managers large and small, or the performance of the median active manager.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Sharpe's summary position is that, properly measured, the average actively managed dollar must underperform the average passively managed dollar net of costs, and that empirical analyses appearing to refute this principle are guilty of improper measurement rather than evidence against it.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. Equally weighting active managers introduces a small-capitalization bias, because equity fund managers with less money under management tend to favour stocks with smaller outstanding values. The 'average active manager' so measured is therefore beaten badly when small-cap stocks underperform large-caps and may exceed the market when small-caps do well -- yet in both cases the average actively managed dollar still underperforms the market net of costs.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 3. Sharpe's performance-measurement rules and the scope of the result

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-003-r1` v2

**Slug** `sharpe-s-performance-measurement-rules-and-the-scope-of-the-result`

### Summary

What follows for practice: peer-group comparisons are a poor basis for decisions, a manager should be measured against a feasible passive benchmark identified in advance, and the constraint binds the dollar-weighted average rather than any individual manager.

### Assertions (3)

1. Sharpe's prescription is that a manager's performance be measured against a comparable passive alternative -- a benchmark or 'normal portfolio' -- which must be a feasible alternative identified in advance of the period being measured. Only under that kind of measurement can an active manager, or someone who hires them, know whether they are in the minority who have beaten viable passive alternatives.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. A second form of the unrepresentative-active-set problem arises when active managers hold securities from outside the market being used as the comparison. Returns on equity mutual funds that hold cash are often compared with an all-equity index, and such funds are generally beaten badly by the index in up markets while sometimes exceeding it in down markets -- an artifact of the mismatch, not evidence about skill.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. Two apparent workarounds to the peer-group problem are rejected: investing equal amounts with many managers is not a practical alternative, and investing with the 'median' manager is less practical still, since that manager's identity is not even known in advance.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`
