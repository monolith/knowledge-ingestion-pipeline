## 1. NYSE 1926-1982: extreme prior-loser portfolios outperformed prior winners over the following three years

`create` · knowledge state **supported** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-001-r1` v2

**Slug** `nyse-1926-1982-extreme-prior-loser-portfolios-outperformed-prior-winners-over-th`

### Summary

The headline result and what qualifies it: a 24.6 per cent spread at 36 months (t = 2.20) averaged over sixteen non-overlapping three-year periods, scaling with the extremeness of the formation period and absent entirely at one year, with the losers carrying lower CAPM betas than the winners.

### Assertions (7)

1. The headline result, averaged over sixteen three-year test periods between 1933 and 1980, is that loser portfolios of 35 stocks outperform the market by 19.6% thirty-six months after portfolio formation while winner portfolios earn about 5.0% less than the market, a difference in cumulative average residual of 24.6% with a t-statistic of 2.20.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The overreaction effect is asymmetric: it is substantially larger for losers than for winners, with losers gaining 19.6% against the market while winners lose only about 5.0%. The authors later attribute this asymmetry partly to a systematic bias in the residual calculation rather than to the phenomenon itself.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The reversal appears late rather than immediately: twelve months into the test period the difference in performance between the extreme portfolios is only 5.4% and statistically insignificant (t = 0.77), and the overreaction phenomenon mostly occurs during the second and third years -- in surprising agreement with Graham's claim that undervaluation takes roughly one and a half to two and a half years to correct.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. The loser portfolios are not merely higher-returning but also lower-risk on the CAPM's own terms: across the experiments the winner portfolios' average betas are significantly larger than the losers', 1.369 against 1.026 for the three-year experiment (t = 3.09 on the difference). Because the market-adjusted residual calculation assumes a beta of 1.00 for both, the reported results underestimate both the magnitude and the statistical significance of the overreaction effect.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. The paper's conclusion is that portfolios of prior losers outperform prior winners by about 25% in the thirty-six months after portfolio formation, even though the winners are significantly more risky -- a result consistent with the overreaction hypothesis and inconsistent with weak-form market efficiency.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The overreaction hypothesis yields two testable predictions about prices alone: (1) extreme stock price movements will be followed by subsequent movements in the opposite direction, and (2) the more extreme the initial movement, the greater the subsequent adjustment. Both imply a violation of weak-form market efficiency, because the reversal is predictable from past return data alone with no use of accounting data such as earnings.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. The second hypothesis is confirmed by varying the formation period: as the cumulative average residuals during the formation period grow larger, the subsequent price reversals grow larger too, along with their t-statistics. At the short extreme this fails to appear at all -- for a formation period as short as one year, no reversal is observed.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 2. Testing overreaction by forming portfolios on past returns rather than on an event

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-002-r1` v2

**Slug** `testing-overreaction-by-forming-portfolios-on-past-returns-rather-than-on-an-eve`

### Summary

The design: portfolios conditioned on past excess returns rather than on a firm-generated event, CRSP monthly NYSE data 1926-1982, an 85-month data requirement that biases toward large firms, three residual types, and the survivorship treatment.

### Assertions (10)

1. The design's defining choice is that portfolios are formed conditional on past excess returns rather than on a firm-generated informational event such as an earnings announcement. Winner and loser portfolios are built from stocks that experienced extreme capital gains or extreme losses over periods up to five years, and the test asks whether residual returns after formation are associated with residual returns before it.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The efficient markets condition is that the expected residual return, conditional on the full information set at t-1, is zero for both portfolios. The overreaction hypothesis instead predicts that this expectation is negative for the winner portfolio and positive for the loser portfolio -- a signed, directional prediction rather than merely a claim of nonzero residuals.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. Portfolios are formed by ranking, at each formation date, the cumulative excess returns of every qualifying stock over the prior 36 months and assigning the top 35 stocks (or top 50, or top decile) to the winner portfolio and the bottom 35 (or 50, or decile) to the loser portfolio. The procedure is repeated across 16 nonoverlapping three-year periods with formation dates from December 1932 to December 1977.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. Each stock must have at least 85 consecutive months of return data to enter the sample, which biases selection toward large, established firms. The authors treat this as strengthening rather than weakening the result: if the overreaction effect holds among large firms, it counters the predictable objection that the effect is mostly a small-firm phenomenon. Between 347 and 1,089 NYSE stocks participate in the various replications.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. Using market-adjusted excess returns biases the research design AGAINST the overreaction hypothesis, so the reported effects understate rather than overstate the phenomenon. The residual is computed as the raw return minus the market return, with no risk adjustment beyond the market's own movement and the same adjustment applied to every stock.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The 36-month test period is a compromise between having enough independent replications and covering a span long enough to matter for asset pricing theory, and it coincides with Benjamin Graham's contention that a substantial undervaluation takes on average about one and a half to two and a half years to correct itself. The number of independent replications varies inversely with the length of the formation period.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. December was chosen as the portfolio formation month, and therefore January as the starting month, essentially arbitrarily; some tests use May as the formation month to check whether the choice affects the results. This matters because the study's most striking finding is a January effect, which the formation-month choice could otherwise be accused of manufacturing.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

8. The sample is monthly return data for New York Stock Exchange common stocks from the CRSP file, covering January 1926 through December 1982, with an equally weighted arithmetic average return on all CRSP-listed securities serving as the market index.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

9. Three types of return residual were computed -- market-adjusted excess returns, market model residuals, and excess returns relative to the Sharpe-Lintner CAPM -- and the results are similar whichever is used, so only market-adjusted excess returns are reported. Because all three are single-index models derived from the CAPM, misspecification could still confound the results.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

10. Survivorship bias from the extreme portfolios' attrition is addressed by how CRSP handles delisting: when a security is delisted, suspended or halted, CRSP determines whether trading at the last listed price is possible, seeks a subsequent quote to compute a final-period return, and enters a return of minus one when shareholders receive nothing. When a stock drops out mid-test, it is permanently removed and the CAR averages the available residuals, which amounts to an implicit rebalancing.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 3. Unexplained: loser-portfolio January excess returns persisting five years after formation

`create` · knowledge state **preliminary** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-003-r1` v2

**Slug** `unexplained-loser-portfolio-january-excess-returns-persisting-five-years-after-f`

### Summary

Most of the effect is realised in January and the authors state plainly that they cannot explain it. It also poses two problems for the tax-loss-selling account, and bears on how the small-firm effect should be read.

### Assertions (9)

1. The price-ratio hypothesis is the behavioral alternative to the artifact reading of the P/E anomaly: very low P/E companies are temporarily undervalued because investors turn excessively pessimistic after a run of bad news, and the price corrects once earnings prove better than the gloomy forecasts; very high P/E equity is correspondingly overvalued before predictably falling.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. The excess returns are concentrated in January: the loser portfolio earns excess returns of 8.1% in month 1 (t = 3.21), 5.6% in month 13 (t = 3.07) and 4.0% in month 25 (t = 2.76) -- that is, in the January following each of the first three years after portfolio formation.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. The results support redefining the small firm effect as a losing-firm effect around the turn of the year: losers persistently earn exceptionally large January returns while winners do not. Crucially, the companies in the extreme portfolios do NOT systematically differ with respect to market capitalization, so firm size cannot be what is driving the difference.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. The findings raise two unanswered questions for the tax-loss selling explanation of the January effect. First, if selling pressure disappears in early January and prices rebound to equilibrium, why does the loser portfolio rebound again in the second, third and fourth Januaries of the test period? Second, why is the January rebound so much larger in magnitude than the selling pressure that supposedly caused it?

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. The results support the price-ratio hypothesis -- that high-P/E stocks are overvalued and low-P/E stocks undervalued -- but that support carries an untested implication: the P/E effect would then also have to be, for the most part, a January phenomenon. No evidence currently supports that claim, apart from the persistent positive relationship between dividend yield, which correlates with the P/E ratio, and January excess returns.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The overreaction phenomenon is not simply the January effect under another name: throughout the test period the difference in cumulative average residual for the three-year formation experiment exceeds that for the two- and one-year experiments, even though all three are visibly affected by the same underlying seasonal pattern.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. In a five-year-long test with 46 yearly replications, the January excess return on the loser portfolio is still observed as late as the fifth January after portfolio formation. Because the 46 successive yearly selections are not independent, no statistical tests are performed on this experiment; its purpose is to remove random noise by multiplying replications.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

8. The loser portfolio tends to decline in value relative to the market between October and December, while the winner portfolio gains value at the end of the year and loses some in January. The authors read the loser pattern as agreeing with the naive version of the tax-loss selling hypothesis.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

9. The authors record that several aspects of their results remain without adequate explanation, most importantly the large positive excess returns the loser portfolio earns every January -- an effect they describe as observed, to their surprise, as late as five years after portfolio formation.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

## 4. The prior literature De Bondt and Thaler argue from and against

`create` · knowledge state **authoritative** · status `ready` · candidate `cand-004-r1` v2

**Slug** `the-prior-literature-de-bondt-and-thaler-argue-from-and-against`

### Summary

The psychological premise the study imports, the earlier observations of market overreaction, the two existing anomalies it cites, and the arbitrage objection it answers by citation.

### Assertions (9)

1. Calling a market reaction an overreaction presupposes a norm for the correct reaction, and the norm De Bondt and Thaler adopt is Bayes' rule, which prescribes how beliefs should be revised in light of new information. This is what lets a psychological finding be turned into a testable market claim: without a normative benchmark, 'too much' reaction has no meaning.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

2. People systematically violate Bayes' rule when revising beliefs: they overweight recent information and underweight prior or base-rate data. Kahneman and Tversky attribute this to a matching rule, in which a case's predicted value is chosen so that its standing among outcomes matches its standing among impressions -- an instance of the representativeness heuristic, which violates the principle that extreme predictions must be moderated by how predictable the outcome actually is.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

3. Shiller's excess-volatility finding is that, over at least the last century, dividends do not vary enough to rationally justify observed aggregate price movements. Combined with Kleidon's result that price movements correlate strongly with the FOLLOWING year's earnings changes, the pattern reads as overreaction: despite the observed trendiness of dividends, investors attach disproportionate importance to short-run economic developments.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

4. The price-earnings ratio anomaly is the observation that stocks in the lowest P/E decile earn larger risk-adjusted returns than high-P/E stocks. Most financial economists treat it as a statistical artifact caused by misspecification of the capital asset pricing model -- typically omitted risk factors for which the P/E ratio is a proxy.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

5. The omitted-risk-factor defense of the P/E anomaly is untestable as usually stated: unless the omitted factors are actually identified, the hypothesis cannot be checked. Reinganum's claim that the small-firm effect subsumes the P/E effect faces direct counter-evidence -- Basu found a significant P/E effect after controlling for firm size, and Graham found the effect within the thirty Dow Jones Industrials, which are not small firms.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

6. The base-rate violation is not an artifact of unmotivated subjects: Grether replicated the finding under incentive-compatible conditions, and professional security analysts and economic forecasters display the same overreaction bias in their actual expectations.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

7. Observations that markets overreact long predate the behavioral literature: Keynes held that ephemeral day-to-day profit fluctuations have an absurd influence on the market, and Williams argued that prices rest too much on current earning power and too little on long-term dividend paying power.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

8. The presence of rational arbitrageurs does not by itself dispose of the overreaction hypothesis. Russell and Thaler conclude that the existence of some rational agents is not sufficient to guarantee a rational expectations equilibrium in an economy that also contains quasi-rational agents -- agents who fail to revise expectations according to Bayes' rule.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

9. The study's claim to novelty is predictive rather than explanatory: the authors state that these tests are, to their knowledge, the first attempt to use a behavioral principle to predict a NEW market anomaly, rather than to explain an already-known one such as the P/E effect or Shiller's volatility results after the fact.

   *backed by* `asmt-0001`

`behavioral finance`, `excess volatility`, `P/E anomaly`
